"How much does an online store cost?" is one of the questions we get asked the most, and the short answer ("it depends") doesn't help anyone. So here's the long answer: **what you pay for with each option, how to add it up and at what point the better choice changes**.

Spoiler: for many businesses that are just starting out, the right answer is a platform like Tiendanube or Empretienda, not a custom-built store. We explain why, and when that stops being the case.

## What you pay for on a platform

Tiendanube, Empretienda, Shopify and the like give you a store that's ready for you to upload products. What you pay:

1. **The monthly plan.** Prices go up with each plan and are adjusted often, so always check the official pricing page.
2. **A per-sale fee**, on some plans. It's a percentage of each sale that the platform keeps, separate from what the payment provider charges. It's common on the lower plans; on the higher ones, it goes down or disappears.
3. **The apps.** Shipping, invoicing, reviews, abandoned cart, wholesale… Many charge their own monthly subscription. This is the cost that slips by most easily: you install them one at a time and by the end of the year they add up.
4. **The template and the design.** There are free and paid templates, and if you want something different, someone to customize it.

## What you pay for with a custom-built store

A store built from scratch, for your business, is paid for differently:

1. **The initial development.** This is the big cost, and it's paid once.
2. **Hosting and domain.** A monthly cost, usually small for a small business store.
3. **Maintenance.** Updates, changes and support. It can be a subscription or by the hour.
4. **There's no platform per-sale fee**, because there's no platform in between.

## What you pay for with both: the payment provider

Mercado Pago (or whatever payment gateway you use) charges a fee on every sale **with either option**. How much depends, above all, on how many days you're willing to wait to have the money available: the sooner, the more expensive. It's worth checking, because sometimes the difference between instant availability and waiting a few days is bigger than the store plan.

## How to do the math (with an example)

The number that matters isn't "how much it costs per month", but **how much it costs you over a year, based on how much you sell**. Do it with your own numbers. An example with made-up numbers, just to show how it works:

| | Platform | Custom-built |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Plan or hosting per month | 30,000 | 15,000 |
| Apps or maintenance per month | 40,000 | 60,000 |
| Platform per-sale fee | 1% | — |
| Initial development (one-time) | 0 | 3,000,000 |

If you sell **5,000,000 per month**:

- **Platform:** 30,000 + 40,000 + 50,000 (the 1%) = **120,000 per month**, that is, 1,440,000 a year.
- **Custom-built:** 15,000 + 60,000 = **75,000 per month**, that is, 900,000 a year, plus the initial 3,000,000.

With those numbers, the custom-built store **takes about five and a half years** to pay back the development. It's not worth it.

Now, if you sell **30,000,000 per month**, the 1% becomes 300,000 a month and the platform costs 370,000 per month. The difference with the custom-built store is almost 300,000 per month, and the development pays for itself in **less than a year**.

That's how it works: **the more you sell, the more the costs that grow with your sales weigh**, and the faster something of your own pays for itself. Replace the numbers in the example with your actual plan, your apps and your revenue.

## When a platform makes sense

- You're just starting out or you don't know yet how much you'll sell.
- Your catalog is standard: products, variants, stock, shipping.
- You don't have your own system (stock, invoicing, ERP) that the store needs to talk to.
- You'd rather not depend on a developer for day-to-day work.

In these cases, a well-configured platform is the best option, and anyone who tells you otherwise is trying to sell you something.

## When custom-built makes sense

- You already sell at a volume where fees and apps weigh heavily, like in the example.
- You need something platforms handle poorly: per-customer price lists, wholesale sales with your own terms, quotes, bookings, configurable products.
- The store needs to connect properly to your stock or invoicing system, and integration apps aren't enough or keep breaking.
- You want the store to be a company asset and not depend on a platform's price and rule changes.

## A middle path

It's not all or nothing. Many businesses start on a platform, learn what they sell and how, and only later move to their own store, already knowing exactly what they need. That second store turns out better and cheaper, because it's designed with real data instead of assumptions.
