It's not modesty. An app nobody downloads is money down the drain, and a client who wasted money with us doesn't come back.

## What an app costs beyond building it

The development quote is the part everyone looks at. But a published app has costs that keep going afterward:

- **The store accounts.** Apple charges an annual membership (USD 99 a year for the Apple Developer Program) and Google Play a one-time registration fee (USD 25). If you stop paying Apple's, your app disappears from the App Store.
- **The reviews.** Every new version goes through Apple's and Google's review. An urgent change, like a wrong price or a bug in the cart, doesn't go live right away: it goes live when they approve it.
- **The mandatory updates.** New versions of iOS and Android come out every year, and the stores require you to keep updating the app to stay published. An app nobody touches for two years ends up removed from the store or broken on new phones.
- **The download.** This is the most underestimated cost. To use your app, the customer has to find it, install it, grant it permissions and remember it exists. To get into your website, tapping a link is enough.

## What a website already solves

A well-made website (fast, designed for mobile) does almost everything a shop needs:

- **Catalog and prices** that update instantly, without going through any review.
- **Orders and payments** with Mercado Pago.
- **Bookings and appointments.**
- **Showing up on Google**, something an app doesn't do: nobody finds your business by searching inside the App Store.
- **Sharing by WhatsApp and Instagram** with a link.
- **Installing on the home screen** as if it were an app, with its own icon, if you build it as a progressive web app (PWA). On iPhones, those installed web apps can also send notifications since iOS 16.4.

If your customer buys from you once a month, or every so often, the website wins. They're not going to install an app for that.

## When an app does make sense

There are cases where a native app is the right answer, and there you shouldn't cut corners:

- **Frequent use.** If the user opens your service several times a week (ordering food, logging hours, tracking a delivery), having the icon on the phone is worth the download.
- **Your team uses it, not your customers.** Delivery drivers, field technicians, salespeople: people you can ask to install something, and who use it every day.
- **Working without internet.** Entering data in a field, a warehouse or a construction site with no signal, and syncing it later.
- **Phone hardware.** Bluetooth with a scale or a printer, NFC, intensive code scanning, GPS in the background.
- **Notifications that are the product.** If the value is in alerting people at exactly the right moment ("your order has shipped," "it's your turn") and your users are repeat users.

## Five questions to decide

1. **How often will the same person use it?** Less than once a week: website.
2. **Who uses it: new customers or people you already know?** If you need people to find you, website first.
3. **Do you need something from the phone that a website can't use?** If the answer is "I don't know," probably not.
4. **Do you have a budget to maintain it every year**, and not just to build it?
5. **Can you test the idea with a website first?** Almost always you can. And if it works, the app gets built later, with real data on what people use.

If you answered "website" on three or more, start there. It's not a consolation prize: it's the path with the least risk and the one that shows you fastest whether the idea works.

## Be wary of anyone who says yes to everything

When you ask a provider for an app, the easy answer is "sure, let's do it." It's more money for them. The useful answer is a question: *what do you need it for?* If nobody asked you, ask yourself.
